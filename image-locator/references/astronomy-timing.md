# Astronomy and timing

Use this branch when a visible astronomical object, credible cast shadow or horizon can distinguish candidate directions or times. Record date, location hypothesis, clock basis, UTC conversion, time zone and daylight-saving assumption. If capture time is unknown, search a **range of dates and times** for each candidate rather than fitting one sunset. Repeat plausible time-zone, clock-error and date hypotheses, then compare the full allowed solar bearing and altitude ranges with the image.

The [USNO Sun/Moon altitude and azimuth calculator](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/AltAz) gives azimuth clockwise from **true north** and altitude of the apparent disk center including standard atmospheric refraction above the horizon. Enter the actual local offset; USNO says its location helper uses current standard zones and does not track historical changes. A geometric model and apparent altitude differ near the horizon. [NOAA's published solar equations](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/solareqns.PDF) provide a reproducible alternative; its [calculator notes](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/calcdetails.html) describe accuracy and refraction limits and say that calculator is no longer maintained.

On level ground, a vertical object's shadow points opposite the Sun's azimuth and has `shadow length / object height = 1 / tan(solar elevation)`. Check that the object is vertical, its base and shadow tip are visible, and the surface is level; slope and unknown height widen the result. A terrain horizon, buildings, haze, cloud, refraction and the solar disk alter apparent rise or set. Camera tilt, crop, field of view, distortion and mirroring affect image-based directions. State the uncertainty interval before comparing sites.

Moon and star positions can also test orientation or time when the body is clearly identified and date, location and exposure are constrained. Use a documented ephemeris such as the USNO calculator for the Moon; compare several bodies or a recognizable star pattern when possible. A single bright point or sunset is usually nonunique. Report astronomical agreement as a constraint on candidate geometry or chronology, not a camera coordinate by itself.
