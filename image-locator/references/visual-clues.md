# Visual clues and image history

## Start from the pixels

Make a compact observation list before searching: literal text and script, sign shapes, building materials and rooflines, lane markings and road direction, shoreline curvature, mountains and skyline, vegetation, snow or leaf state, light and shadows, reflections and occlusion. Keep **observed pixels** ("white edge dashes, concrete poles, road bends right beyond a junction") separate from **interpretations** ("northern country, route X, town Y"). Mark uncertain readings. The [Berkeley Protocol's verification guidance](https://humanrights.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Berkeley-Protocol.pdf) treats geolocation and chronolocation as checks against other evidence.

Use distinctive road markings, bollards, pole design, driving side, vegetation, soil and architecture to propose several regions, checking whether the features actually co-occur there. These are search priors, not camera coordinates: local variation, imported materials, season and image date can defeat a single cue. [Rainbolt's GeoGuessr walkthrough](https://www.wired.com/video/watch/wired-news-and-science-how-a-pro-geoguessr-guesses-in-0-dot-1-seconds) illustrates the progression from road and landscape clues to mapped road angle, curves and signage. Vehicle body, camera mount, blur or coverage-era artifacts from Street View are relevant only when the **supplied image itself** is Street View; ordinary photographs cannot inherit Street View capture clues from a comparison map.

Sketch a simple top-down layout before searching closely: camera-facing road direction, near and far turns, junction arms, landmark order and side of road, skyline or ridge shape, and occluding objects. Keep compass heading tentative until independently fixed; a mirrored image reverses left and right. Within each candidate region search maps, aerial imagery and street-level imagery for the **combination** of road curves, junction shape, signs and fixed landmarks. Record candidates that match the combination and those that almost match. Bellingcat's [road-and-mosque example](https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2014/07/09/a-beginners-guide-to-geolocation/) uses the median and road width to find a route, then minaret order, road curvature, trees and the absence of taller buildings to test the proposed viewpoint. Take a matching feature combination to [camera geometry](map-geometry.md); a regional cue alone stays labelled as a region selector.

For small text, inspect a crop from a copy, increase contrast or deskew as needed, and try OCR with an appropriate layout mode. Recheck ambiguous characters in the original pixels, especially where a guessed place name would decide the search. [Tesseract's quality guide](https://tesseract-ocr.github.io/tessdoc/ImproveQuality.html) documents resolution, preprocessing and page segmentation effects. Search exact phrases, spelling variants and combinations of unrelated clues. Keep at least two plausible candidate regions until fixed geometry rules them out.

## Trace image history when authorized

With user authorization to disclose the image or crop to a third party, reverse-search the full image and distinctive crops. [Google Lens](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/1325808) can return similar images and pages containing an image; [About this image](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/14177408) can show available context. Inspect the earliest accessible **original page and image**, its publication date and authorship; repeated copies or captions are one lineage of evidence. The earliest indexed appearance bounds known publication history, not capture time. A crop can match a photographed landmark while leaving the camera site unresolved.

Without upload authorization, use text searches, public maps and pages about visible features. If the image is already publicly hosted, note whether querying its URL would disclose it to a service before doing so. Record which search route was used and what it actually established.
