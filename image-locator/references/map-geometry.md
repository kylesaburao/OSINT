# Maps and camera-site geometry

## Compare candidates

For each plausible site, identify fixed landmarks in the image and on independent maps or original venue or municipal sources. Place the candidate camera on the mapped road, path or accessible structure; face it toward the scene and compare the observed sequence of bends, junction arms, road-edge convergence, signs, landmark order and near-object occlusion. Try nearby positions, both travel directions and plausible camera heights. Compare at least two distinct constraints: landmark order or angular separation, road or coast shape, roof or window pattern, occlusion, terrain skyline, or a feasible height. A candidate that explains only the named subject is incomplete. Bellingcat's [map-matching example](https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2014/07/09/a-beginners-guide-to-geolocation/) checks road shape, landmark orientation, trees and potentially missing tall buildings. An address or map pin for a landmark is a **subject** coordinate until a sightline locates the camera.

Sketch the projected sightlines from each candidate and ask which visible fixed feature should appear but does not, or should be occluded but is not. Treat map geometry and street imagery as dated measurements; altered roads, vegetation, buildings or imagery capture positions can explain a mismatch. A crop hides context, and a mirror reverses lateral order and inferred heading. Determine the transforms when possible; otherwise keep heading and calibrated measurements conditional. If several road segments or viewpoints survive, report their bounded extent or separate alternatives rather than selecting the best-looking pin.

For two mapped landmarks, calculate forward azimuth and distance from each proposed camera area with the [GeographicLib WGS84 inverse geodesic](https://geographiclib.sourceforge.io/html/python/geodesics.html) or a documented equivalent. Compare the predicted bearing order and separation to the image. Intersect bearing bands from independent landmarks rather than treating one ray as a unique point. A horizontal pixel order remains useful when calibration is absent, but it supports less precision.

## Calibrated field of view

When two or more fixed landmarks are resolved in the pixels, camera calibration can turn their image separation into an angular constraint. Prefer intrinsics tied to the **delivered image**: pixel focal lengths `fx`, `fy`, principal point `cx`, `cy`, and distortion parameters. For an undistorted, level, rectilinear image with no roll, a landmark at horizontal pixel `x` has a camera-relative direction `theta(x) = atan((x - cx) / fx)`; subtract two such angles to compare with the candidate site's landmark-bearing difference. Absolute heading remains unknown until a mapped feature or reliable orientation fixes it. For tilted images or landmarks at different elevations, use the full 2D camera rays and pose instead of this horizontal shortcut. [OpenCV's camera model](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html) defines the intrinsics, projection and distortion terms.

If intrinsics are absent but the actual focal length `f` and **active** sensor width are known, a rectilinear horizontal field of view is `2 atan(active width / (2f))`. A 35 mm equivalent focal length can give an approximate diagonal field of view, but its conversion to this image's horizontal field depends on the captured aspect ratio and crop; do not treat it as physical `f`. [Apple describes its nominal 35 mm equivalent as a diagonal field-of-view measure](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/avcapturedevice/nominalfocallengthin35mmfilm). A model's advertised lens specification is a weaker prior than calibration stored for the actual capture.

Before comparing angular separations, reconcile metadata with the **displayed raster**: orientation, digital zoom, crop offsets, resize, perspective or panorama stitching, lens correction, and any mirroring. For an unscaled crop whose left edge was `L` pixels from the calibrated image, use `cx_new = cx - L` and the same `fx`; a later scale by `s` multiplies both by `s`. Crop width changes total field of view but preserves the separation of retained rays. Undistort measured points with the matching calibration. [OpenCV documents scaling intrinsics and undistorting points](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html). Test a plausible range of effective field of view and point-picking error for each candidate. If calibration is unavailable, several independently mapped landmarks can estimate an effective field of view, but validate the fit with a landmark held out of that estimate. Record the residual mismatch and keep metadata-based geometry conditional until independent pixel geometry corroborates it.

## Conditional perspective and terrain tests

- **Vanishing directions.** If image edges are genuinely parallel in the world (for example, straight road edges or parallel facade lines), extend each set to a vanishing point or line. Compare the resulting axis or horizon with candidate road or building orientation. This constrains direction and perspective; without a mapped axis and reliable calibration it does not give a camera coordinate. Curved roads, sloped or nonparallel edges, rolling shutter and stitched images can produce misleading intersections. [Single-view metrology](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/single-view-metrology-4/) derives scene measurements from vanishing geometry under explicit reference-plane assumptions.
- **Skyline and viewshed.** For a visible ridge, compare the order and profile of peaks, using angular spacing only when calibration supports it. Test a range of candidate camera positions and observer heights with a DEM whose horizontal and vertical resolution can resolve the features. Compare foreground occlusion separately: a terrain DEM omits buildings and trees. [GDAL's viewshed settings](https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_raster_viewshed.html) include observer and target height, distance limits, and curvature/refraction; document those settings and vary uncertain heights. [USGS's National Map Viewer](https://www.usgs.gov/tools/national-map-viewer) provides 3DEP data for US scenes, subject to local coverage and resolution. A similar ridge profile is a constraint, not a unique point.
- **Planar homography.** If identifiable control features lie on **one plane** (a flat facade, sign or road patch), match at least four well-spread, non-collinear points between the image and an independently sourced plan or comparison view. Fit a homography to test that plane's layout or rectify it for comparison. It does not map off-plane objects correctly or by itself establish absolute camera position; metric measurements also require a known scale. Check unused points on the same plane and reject a fit that only explains its selected controls. For camera pose from coplanar points, calibration and possible pose ambiguities matter; [OpenCV's homography guide](https://docs.opencv.org/3.4.8/d9/dab/tutorial_homography.html) recommends `solvePnP` for pose estimation.
- **Calibrated 2D-to-3D pose.** If several reliable, spatially spread image features have independently mapped 3D coordinates or heights and the delivered raster's intrinsics and distortion are known, fit candidate rotation and translation with a calibrated PnP method. Convert the fitted world-to-camera pose to a camera position in the map frame, then test the orientation and physical accessibility. [OpenCV's PnP documentation](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/d5/d1f/calib3d_solvePnP.html) defines the 3D-to-2D correspondences, camera model and reprojection objective. A low training residual alone is weak: wrong feature matches, clustered or coplanar controls, map-height errors, crop/mirror mistakes and lens mismatch can give a plausible but displaced pose. Hold out independent mapped features for reprojection, inspect residuals across the frame, and vary calibration, picked pixels and map heights before a fitted pose can justify a precise camera point. If the checks fail or controls are sparse, report an area.

When distinct supplied viewpoints exist, parallax between them may add depth constraints after their relationship is established; a single photo supplies no such baseline. Record which observed line or silhouette each method tests and the contradiction it would expose.
